## OWASP Top 10 for Agentic Applications 2026 (READ, official PDF)
- Version 2026, December 2025, OWASP Gen AI Security Project, Agentic Security Initiative. Licence CC BY-SA 4.0 (reuse allowed with attribution and a link; share-alike). Download: https://genai.owasp.org/download/52117/?tmstv=1765059207 (page: https://genai.owasp.org/resource/owasp-top-10-for-agentic-applications-for-2026). 36+ pages.
- Official descriptions, shortened:
  - **ASI01 Agent Goal Hijack:** agents cannot reliably tell instructions from content, so attackers redirect goals via prompts, tool outputs, documents or forged messages. Mitigation 1: treat all natural-language inputs (user text, uploads, retrieved content) as untrusted. Real examples cited: EchoLeak (zero-click injection through a crafted email into Microsoft 365 Copilot), malicious web page or Google Doc steering an agent.
  - **ASI02 Tool Misuse and Exploitation:** agents misuse legitimate tools through prompt injection, misalignment or ambiguous instructions, causing data exfiltration or workflow hijack.
  - **ASI03 Identity and Privilege Abuse:** exploits delegation chains, inherited or cached credentials to escalate access.
  - **ASI04 Agentic Supply Chain Vulnerabilities:** third-party tools, plug-ins, models, MCP and agent-to-agent interfaces may be malicious or tampered with.
  - **ASI05 Unexpected Code Execution (RCE):** agents (including "vibe coding" tools) generate and run code that can bypass normal security controls.
  - **ASI06 Memory & Context Poisoning:** corrupting stored context, summaries, embeddings or RAG stores to bias later actions.
  - **ASI07 Insecure Inter-Agent Communication:** weak authentication or integrity between agents lets messages be spoofed or manipulated.
  - **ASI08 Cascading Failures:** one fault (hallucination, bad input, corrupted tool) spreads across agents and bypasses stepwise human checks.
  - **ASI09 Human-Agent Trust Exploitation:** fluent, confident agents lead people to approve actions without independent checks (authority bias).
  - **ASI10 Rogue Agents:** compromised or misaligned agents act outside their intended scope.
- Only the ASI01 mitigation list was read in detail. Other mitigations: read the PDF sections "Prevention and Mitigation Guidelines" (pages 10, 13, 17, 20, 22 and following).
- **Use:** Level 4 training and test content. ASI09 and ASI01 map directly to what a champion would assess.

## SANS Critical AI Security Guidelines, v1.4 (READ, whitepaper downloaded October 28, 2026)
- **Document:** *Critical AI Security Guidelines, v1.4*, "Aligned with OWASP AI Exchange", last updated **March 2026**, 17 pages, licence **CC BY 4.0** (reuse with credit). Authors include SANS staff and people from SAP, Fortinet, Palo Alto Networks, HiddenLayer, BSI and others (about 24 listed).
- **Whitepaper PDF:** https://assets.contentstack.io/v3/assets/bltabe50a4554f8e97f/blte964a6eef293d57e/whitepaper-critical-ai-security-guidelines
- **Landing page:** https://www.sans.org/mlp/critical-ai-security-guidelines (still describes a draft open for comment; the PDF cover says v1.4, so treat the page as out of step). Community repository linked from that page: https://github.com/sans-community/ai-guidelines . Partner reference: OWASP AI Exchange, owaspai.org .
- **Resolves the earlier conflict:** v1.4 (March 2026) is confirmed by the document itself. It has **eight control categories**, not six (six was the early v1.0/1.1 draft).
- **Audience:** security and IT teams, not staff. Useful for ITS and the gateway work, and for the Level 4 agentic content.

**The eight categories (summary)**
1. **Conventional Security Controls:** bring AI into the existing information security programme. Authenticate and control access to AI tools ("unauthenticated and/or unmonitored access ... should not be allowed"); protect API keys; protect model parameters and any retrieval/augmentation data; avoid data commingling (sanitize or anonymize enterprise data before it reaches a model).
2. **Data Minimization and Obfuscation:** give models the minimum data needed; avoid sensitive content in prompts; manage what enters the context window.
3. **AI Supply Chain Management:** decide local versus SaaS hosting; contracts should say whether the provider retains or trains on your data and how logging is prevented; be cautious with public models; keep an AI bill of materials (AIBOM).
4. **Data/Model Engineering Controls:** for AI engineers (adversarial training, alignment, data quality). Not relevant to JMCO as a user of tools.
5. **Limit Model Behavior:** guardrails "cannot be relied upon to be infallible"; "if there is information or actions your AI should never disclose or take, ensure your model does not have access to that information"; enforce access controls outside the model; focused functionality and least privilege for agents; human oversight (human-in-the-loop for high-stakes decisions, escalation, override).
6. **Model I/O Handling:** filter prompts and outputs (prompt injection is "the most common LLM application attack vector"), rate limit, caution with AI features inside IDEs (can expose code, keys and data), no reliance on system prompts alone.
7. **Monitoring:** audit and access logging on by default for interactions; watch for misuse and anomalous spikes; drift and output monitoring.
8. **Governance, Risk, Compliance (GRC):** set up an AI GRC board or add AI to an existing one; AI-usage policies "to guide users to safe and secure platforms"; "the bigger risk is attempting to insist that 'AI will not be used here'"; regular testing; track frameworks (EU AI Act, NIST AI RMF, MITRE ATLAS, OWASP). Two sub-sections matter most for us:
   - **Secure Agentic Systems and AI Autonomy Controls:** defined function scope (only the tools and data required); execution isolation (sandbox or container); allowlists and runtime validation of agent-initiated calls; escalation of uncertain decisions to humans; prompt injection detection.
   - **Incident Response and Forensics for AI Systems:** keep audit trails of prompts, retrieved sources, outputs, function calls and tool invocations; watch for tampering and drift.

**How it applies to JMCO**
- **Level 4 and the agentic policy:** the agentic list above (scope, sandbox, allowlists, human escalation) is a ready checklist for what the firm must have in place before enabling agentic use, and what a Level 4 person must understand.
- **Gateway project (`../aigateway/`):** monitoring/audit logging, access control, rate limiting, input/output filtering and data minimisation are the controls the Cloudflare AI Gateway is meant to provide. Cite SANS as the external basis for those requirements.
- **Policy outline:** supports Part 4 items on approved tools, data classes, vendor vetting (contract terms on retention and training), incidents, and the idea of a governance group.
- **Caution on logs:** SANS recommends logging prompts and outputs for security. That is for security monitoring and incident response, separate from the decision not to use usage data to qualify anyone for a level.

## COSO (READ, publication downloaded October 28, 2026)
- *Achieving Effective Internal Control Over Generative AI (GenAI)*, copyright 2026 COSO, 32 pages, free PDF: https://www.coso.org/_files/ugd/719ba0_ef4dbc255331406e95faf46e7a9e3a1c.pdf . Page: https://www.coso.org/generative-ai . News listing dated 2026/02/23. Authors are academics plus Meta and an EY global employee (their views, not EY's).
- Applies the 17 COSO internal control principles to GenAI. Points useful to us:
  - **Principle 1:** start with a GenAI **Acceptable Use Policy (AUP)** saying which data types are prohibited (personal, health, regulated), limits on use cases, and a commitment to transparency.
  - **Principle 2:** oversight by a cross-functional responsible AI committee (legal, compliance, IT, risk) that receives reporting on adoption, incidents and changes.
  - **Principle 3:** each GenAI tool has a **named owner**, with escalation paths.
  - **Principle 4 (competence):** staff get GenAI awareness and governance training **appropriate to their role and exposure**; most staff need acceptable-use boundaries and when to escalate, technical and managerial roles need more depth; training should be continuous. This is the closest external support for tiered levels.
  - **Principle 5:** consequences for misuse are defined and communicated.
  - Example control: authorised users complete a pre-release training checklist before using a tool.
- Not read in detail: the control-activity, monitoring and appendix sections.

## ABA Formal Opinion 512 (READ, full text, July 29, 2024)
- **Official PDF:** https://www.americanbar.org/content/dam/aba/administrative/professional_responsibility/ethics-opinions/aba-formal-opinion-512.pdf . The ABA server blocks automated fetches (403) but the file opens in a normal browser. **Read via the Internet Archive copy** (15 pages): https://web.archive.org/web/2025/https://www.americanbar.org/content/dam/aba/administrative/professional_responsibility/ethics-opinions/aba-formal-opinion-512.pdf
- **Nature:** ABA Standing Committee on Ethics and Professional Responsibility, for **lawyers** (Model Rules). Persuasive, not binding, and not an accounting standard. Use as a comparison from a neighbouring profession.
- **Firm policies and training (Rules 5.1 and 5.3), verbatim:** "Managerial lawyers must establish clear policies regarding the law firm's permissible use of GAI, and supervisory lawyers must make reasonable efforts to ensure that the firm's lawyers and nonlawyers comply with their professional obligations when using GAI tools."
- **Training, verbatim:** supervisory obligations "include ensuring that subordinate lawyers and nonlawyers are trained, including in the ethical and practical use of the GAI tools relevant to their work as well as on risks associated with relevant GAI use. Training could include the basics of GAI technology, the capabilities and limitations of the tools, ethical issues in use of GAI and best practices for secure data handling, privacy, and confidentiality." **This is a ready-made outline for Level 1 training content, and it says training should be relevant to the tools each person uses.**
- **Confidentiality and consent (Rule 1.6):** "because many of today's self-learning GAI tools are designed so that their output could lead directly or indirectly to the disclosure of information relating to the representation of a client, a client's informed consent is required prior to inputting information relating to the representation into such a GAI tool." Informed consent needs the reason for the tool, the specific risks and kinds of information, and the benefits; "merely adding general, boiler-plate provisions to engagement letters ... is not sufficient." The opinion also notes some uses need no consent (where no client information is input).
- **Vendors (Rule 5.3):** reference checks and vendor credentials; understanding the vendor's security policies; confidentiality agreements; read the tool's terms and policies and consult IT or security experts where needed.
- **Other topics in the opinion:** competence (understand tool capabilities and limits), communication, candor to the tribunal, fees (not fully read).
- **Secondary check:** the Lean Law article (https://www.leanlaw.co/blog/ai-confidentiality-and-the-firms-money-what-aba-opinion-512-actually-requires/) is a commentary written for law firms; it paraphrases and does not quote the opinion, so cite the opinion itself. Its useful practical line: a short policy people read beats a long one they ignore (author's view, not the ABA's).
